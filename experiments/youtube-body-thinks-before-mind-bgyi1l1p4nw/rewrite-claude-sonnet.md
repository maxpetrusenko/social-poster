# Neuroscientists Have Confirmed Our Suspicions | Pushka. Brain

> This article follows the evidence behind a familiar story, then turns it into a practical way to read the system, its limits, and the decision a careful reader can make next.

This Russian-language science digest covers infrasound, anesthesia, cancer, and spinal repair. Across all four, the same pattern keeps turning up. Hidden control systems act first, and consciousness can only report them afterward.

![Textless editorial image of hidden sound waves moving through the body before conscious awareness.](assets/generated-bitmap-v1/hidden-body-signals-hero-overlay.png)

The first clue in the story is a fencing sword that shakes on its own.

Vic Tandy worked around medical equipment and had little patience for ghost stories. His colleagues said the lab felt wrong. They felt watched, and some saw a grey figure at the edge of their vision.

Tandy laughed at these reports until he stayed late. Dread flooded his body. He saw the same figure at the edge of his vision and began sweating in a cold room. Afterward, a blade held in a vise began to tremble.

![A haunted laboratory becomes an engineering problem.](assets/youtube-frames/selected-v3/01-ghost-machine-paper.jpg)

Tandy moved a table and watched the vibration of the blade rise and fall. That was the clue. The dread in the room traced back to a standing wave of infrasound, a pressure too low for ordinary hearing, produced by the ventilation. It interacted with the geometry of the room and settled into a pattern.

The case became the classic [Tandy and Lawrence "ghost in the machine" account](https://www.richardwiseman.com/resources/ghost-in-machine.pdf). An apparent ghost turned into a measurable waveform.

A newer [double-blind infrasound experiment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full) pushed the same finding further: the body can respond to a signal the mind has not identified. Researchers paired [infrasound near 18 Hz with either calm music or anxious ambient sound](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full).

Participants [guessed at chance level whether the hidden low sound was present](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full). Even so, their [saliva showed a rise in cortisol](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full). Calm music [felt gloomier when infrasound was present](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full). Listeners' [emotional state shifted while they remained consciously unaware of the signal](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full).

![Natural infrasound turns the invisible signal into weather-scale machinery.](assets/youtube-frames/selected-v3/03-spectrogram-response.jpg)

The episode ran in a fixed order. A signal arrived below the level anyone could report, and the organism's state shifted.

Then consciousness wrote a caption for the change. Sometimes the caption blamed a ghost, sometimes a bad mood, and sometimes it offered no explanation at all.

## The hippocampus keeps reading after the lights go out

The older model of the brain gives consciousness a central executive job: it notices, decides, remembers, and explains. A newer view pictures a building at night. The front desk is closed, but some departments keep working.

Seven epilepsy patients received propofol anesthesia during surgery. Neuropixels probes were placed deep enough to record individual cells in the hippocampus. Access like that is rare. A [Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10448-0) reports the study, with a recording window of about ten to twenty minutes per patient.

The study asked what happens when speech and sound enter a brain whose owner will later remember none of it.

![The hippocampus study becomes the hinge of the article.](assets/youtube-frames/selected-v3/04-hippocampus-diagram.jpg)

Patients first heard repeated tones with the occasional deviant oddball mixed in. Hippocampal neurons adapted to the repetition. They learned the pattern well enough to flag the oddballs as exceptions.

Then the patients were played speech. Hippocampal cells tracked the meanings of words, their grammatical roles, and the probability of which word might come next. Words with similar meanings produced related patterns of neural activity. All of this happened while the patients were anesthetized, and they remembered nothing from the speech stream. The hippocampus still processed language in a structured way. A [Scientific American summary](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-brain-processes-overheard-words-under-anesthesia-but-it-may-not-remember-them/) described the result as overheard words being processed under anesthesia without later recall.

![Surgical sleep leaves some high-level processing active.](assets/youtube-frames/selected-v3/05-surgical-sleep-mri.jpg)

The view here is narrower than hidden wakefulness under anesthesia. Some regions may keep processing language while the system that makes experience belong to someone sits idle.

A sentence can be processed without being experienced as one's own.

The anesthesia case says something about the self. Consciousness may be less the part that does the work than a layer that binds distributed processing into one remembered scene.

Switch that binding layer off, and the processing can still go on.

## A dream can spend the night on your behalf

Sleep is supposed to be when the body repairs itself. Some dreams work more like a second job. This episode covers hyperoneirism, also called epic dreaming: long, vivid, continuous dream experiences that leave people exhausted, even when standard sleep measures look normal.

Patients describe whole nights of plots, missions, conversations, travel, conflict, and obligation. They wake feeling as though they have already worked a shift.

![Epic dreaming becomes easier to grasp when it stops being abstract.](assets/youtube-frames/selected-v3/07-epic-dream-illustration.jpg)

A patient can have a normal sleep chart. Breathing looks normal, muscle activity looks normal, and the sleep stages look normal. Even so, the patient can wake up carrying a physical cost from the night.

Medicine often meets a pattern the patient experiences before clinicians have a clean instrument to measure it. In those cases the private report comes first, then classification of the pattern, and only after that, treatment.

Epic dreaming matters because it makes a hidden layer of sleep costly. The body spends the night inside a story, and the waking self inherits the result in the morning.

## Cancer is a control-system failure

The Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial is the next step in the same argument. The piece has moved from perception to tissue. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is a disease where optimism has to be earned in small steps. It is often detected late. It spreads fast. It has resisted many therapies that changed other cancers.

That history is why the daraxonrasib result landed so hard. Daraxonrasib is an oral, multi-selective RAS(ON) inhibitor. In the [Phase 3 RASolute 302 result](https://ir.revmed.com/news-releases/news-release-details/daraxonrasib-demonstrates-unprecedented-overall-survival-benefit), patients with previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer had a median overall survival of about 13.2 months on the drug. Standard chemotherapy in the same trial gave about 6.7 months.

Two institutions read the number the same way. ASCO's [conference release](https://www.asco.org/about-asco/press-center/multi-selective-ras-inhibitor-nearly-doubles-survival-pancreatic-cancer) frames it as a serious survival signal in a very hard disease. So does Dana-Farber's [clinical summary](https://www.dana-farber.org/newsroom/news-releases/2026/rason-inhibitor-doubles-median-overall-survival-in-results-of-phase-3-trial-for-patients-with-metastatic-pancreatic-cancer).

![Textless editorial image of a tumor control network.](assets/generated-bitmap-v1/tumor-control-network.png)

The claim about the treatment has two parts, and both need stating. In this disease, a near doubling of survival is a very large effect. The survival endpoint is still measured in months.

The passage describes the result in sober terms. A handle appeared on a target once considered almost undruggable.

The deeper lesson is about system architecture. Cancer is a broken control system involving growth signals, DNA repair, immune evasion, tissue pressure, nutrient routes, local signaling, and evolutionary selection. A drug matters when it reaches a control node. A small molecule can change a map.

## A tumor can change the neighborhood rules

Chromosomal instability means cells carry abnormal chromosome numbers or structures. It looks like damage, and in cancer it can work in the tumor's favor. Those irregular chromosomes give a tumor variation to search with: extra growth genes, missing suppressors, unstable combinations nobody planned.

The episode's next paper describes another territorial behavior. Some chromosomally unstable tumor cells slip into a senescent-like state. They stop dividing the usual way, but they keep talking. They broadcast signals that push neighboring healthy cells to slow down or die. IRB Barcelona's [study summary](https://www.irbbarcelona.org/en/news/scientific/study-flies-reveals-how-some-tumours-eliminate-healthy-neighbouring-cells-grow) describes the work as a fruit-fly model in which tumors clear room around themselves.

![The tumor paper shifts the story from growth to territory.](assets/youtube-frames/selected-v3/09-embo-tumor-paper.jpg)

A senescent cell is expected to stop dividing, and it does. It stays active, though. Senescent cells make the surrounding environment worse for normal tissue and better for a tumor.

![The animated tumor is crude, but the mechanism is memorable: clear space, gain room.](assets/youtube-frames/selected-v3/10-cancer-cell-graphic.jpg)

The evidence comes from experiments on fruit flies, and whether it holds in humans is still unresolved. The shift in thinking still matters.

The question moves from what is broken inside the malignant cell to how that cell rewrites the rules of the tissue around it. A tumor can behave a bit like poor governance.

## A tiny gland can lock a whole life state

A woman has one child, stops breastfeeding, and then develops secondary infertility. Her body keeps making milk long after she stopped nursing. She also has headaches, night sweats, and fatigue.

Prolactin is a hormone that helps drive lactation. Too much of it can suppress ovulation.

The source here is a pituitary tumor at the base of the brain. Remove it, and prolactin returns to normal levels. Pregnancy becomes possible again.

The case takes up little screen time, but it shows a control system well. A tiny gland can set a reproductive state that reaches the whole body, and a signal for lactation can act as a lock on fertility.

A local mass can become a problem that shapes the course of a person's life. Bodies have dashboards, but people mostly experience only the output.

## Repair may be a locked program

Careless writing is most risky in the spinal-cord section, because the hope it raises is so obvious. Researchers built human organoid circuits that model connections between brain tissue and spinal cord tissue. During development, neurons grow axons with considerable freedom. Mature central nervous system neurons lose much of that ability.

A [Cell Reports study](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247%2826%2900477-8) identified a program that restrains axon regrowth. The same study then screened for ways to loosen it.

![Textless editorial image of axons regrowing across an organoid culture.](assets/generated-bitmap-v1/axon-regrowth-organoid.png)

Lynestrenol is a synthetic progestin used as a contraceptive. In the organoid model, it improved axon growth. The lab's public explanation of the finding was also covered by [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260528082459.htm).

Keep the setting in view. The result comes from a dish model, which is far from an injured human spinal cord. Real injury adds scar tissue, inflammation, and broken tissue architecture. The hardest problem there is reconnecting the right neurons to the right places.

Still, the finding points somewhere useful. Adult neurons may carry a brake on repair.

If that brake can be mapped, therapy becomes less mysterious. It would involve timing, guidance, scaffolding, chemistry, and targeted release of a developmental program. Hope is stronger when it is specific rather than vague.

## The hidden layer keeps winning

The episode is a digest that moves across many topics: a haunted lab, infrasound, anesthesia, dreams, pancreatic cancer, tumor ecology, prolactin, and spinal repair. The common thread is a hidden control layer.

Vibration can alter stress before it is perceived as sound. The hippocampus processes language before memory receives a scene. A dream uses up the night before the waking self can object to it.

A cancer cell changes the rules governing tissue. A pituitary signal suppresses reproduction. A mature neuron has a brake that a developing neuron lacks.

The self that a person can narrate is real but arrives late.

Most of the body runs on older regulatory machinery: sensors, thresholds, switches, chemical signals, emergency policies, developmental locks, local negotiations, and inherited evolutionary leftovers that still operate. Consciousness remains part of the picture, and it becomes more interesting as the layer that makes a world reportable after deeper systems have already acted.

A person is a layered organism. Many layers are already processing information before the conscious person arrives.
